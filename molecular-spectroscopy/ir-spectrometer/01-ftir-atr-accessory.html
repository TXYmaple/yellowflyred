ATR-FTIR Spectroscopy: Principles and Practical Applications

Category: Molecular Spectroscopy • Series: IR Spectrometer • Part 1 • Published: November 2026

Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) is a sampling technique used with Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy to examine solid, liquid, and semi-solid samples with minimal preparation. Since its introduction in the 1960s and its widespread adoption with modern FTIR instruments, ATR has become one of the most common infrared sampling methods in analytical laboratories.

1. Principles of ATR

ATR relies on total internal reflection. An infrared beam enters a high-refractive-index crystal (typically diamond, zinc selenide, germanium, or silicon) at an angle greater than the critical angle. The beam reflects internally within the crystal, creating an evanescent wave that extends a short distance (typically 0.5–2 µm) beyond the crystal surface into the sample.

Where the sample absorbs infrared radiation at specific wavelengths, the evanescent wave is attenuated. The detector records this attenuation as a spectrum, which is characteristic of the sample's molecular structure. Because the evanescent wave penetrates only a few micrometres, the technique is inherently surface-sensitive and requires no sample thinning or KBr pellet preparation.

2. Instrumentation

A typical ATR accessory consists of:

  • ATR crystal — diamond is preferred for hardness and chemical resistance; ZnSe offers a wider spectral range but is softer and sensitive to acidic samples.
  • Sample clamp — ensures uniform contact between sample and crystal.
  • Pressure mechanism — critical for solid samples; poor contact produces weak or distorted spectra.

Modern ATR-FTIR instruments use single-reflection accessories that require only microlitre or milligram quantities of sample. Cleaning between samples is straightforward: the crystal is wiped with a suitable solvent and dried before the next measurement.

3. Advantages and Limitations

Advantages

  • Minimal sample preparation — no KBr pellets, no mulling agents.
  • Rapid measurement — typically under one minute per spectrum.
  • Non-destructive for most samples.
  • Suitable for aqueous solutions, pastes, polymers, and powders.
  • Excellent reproducibility when contact pressure is controlled.

Limitations

  • Strongly absorbing samples (e.g., carbon black-filled polymers) can saturate the evanescent wave.
  • Water absorbs strongly in the mid-IR, limiting path length for aqueous samples.
  • Crystal damage from hard or abrasive samples is a practical concern.
  • Depth of penetration varies with wavelength and refractive index, complicating quantitative analysis.

4. Applications

ATR-FTIR is widely used across analytical and industrial laboratories:

  • Polymers and materials — identification of plastics, rubbers, coatings, and adhesives.
  • Pharmaceuticals — raw material identification, polymorph screening, and counterfeit detection.
  • Forensics — analysis of paints, fibres, drugs, and unknown residues.
  • Food and agriculture — authentication of oils, detection of adulterants, and quality control.
  • Environmental — microplastics identification and soil organic matter characterization.
  • Biomedical — tissue analysis and biofilm studies.

5. Practical Considerations

Successful ATR analysis depends on good contact between sample and crystal. Solid samples should be pressed firmly and uniformly; liquids should fully cover the crystal surface. Background spectra must be collected regularly, and the crystal should be inspected for scratches or residue. For quantitative work, calibration curves should be constructed using the same contact conditions as unknown samples.

Conclusion

ATR-FTIR combines the molecular specificity of infrared spectroscopy with simple, rapid sampling. Its minimal preparation requirements and broad applicability have made it a standard tool in materials characterization, pharmaceutical quality control, forensic analysis, and environmental monitoring. When operated with proper attention to contact, cleanliness, and calibration, ATR provides reliable and reproducible spectra for a wide range of sample types.
